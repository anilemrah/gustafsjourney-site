A Swedish Christmas, from Lucia to Saint Knut's Day

In Sweden, Christmas begins with candles in the dark and ends with a dance round the tree a month later. Here are the traditions Gustaf grew up with in Mora, and the ones Swedish-Americans carried to Minnesota.

God Jul!
13 December

Lucia: light in the long night

In Swedish, ljus means both light and candle, and Lucia is all about both. Under the old calendar, Lucia night was the longest night of the whole year. People stayed awake to keep watch over the farm and the animals, and young people dressed up and went from farm to farm, singing for a bite to eat.

The Lucia we know today, in a white gown with a red sash and candles in her hair, began around Lake Vänern in western Sweden in the late 1700s, and the custom spread slowly. She comes early in the morning, bringing light before the sun is up. Her attendants, the tärnor, wear white too, with candles in their hands, and star boys walk in the procession in tall pointed hats.

The Lucia treat is lussekatter: saffron buns, often curled into an S with raisins. Swedish-Americans still celebrate Lucia today, from Minneapolis to Bemidji.

All of December

Gingerbread and the Christmas goat

Pepparkakor are thin, crisp gingerbread biscuits, cut into hearts, stars and pigs. They are mentioned at a Swedish convent as early as 1444.

The julbock is a Christmas goat made of straw. Before Santa Claus came along, it was the Christmas goat who brought the presents.

24 December

Christmas Eve, the big day

In Sweden the big day is Christmas Eve. The julbord, the Christmas table, is full: ham, sausages, lutfisk, fresh bread and cheese. The ham is boiled, coated with mustard, egg and breadcrumbs and browned in the oven, and the family dips bread in the broth from the pot. Swedes call it dopp i grytan, dip in the pot.

Then comes creamy rice porridge with cinnamon. One almond is hidden in the pot, and whoever finds it, people say, will marry within the year.

Did you know?The tomte is the little farm guardian of Swedish folklore. Viktor Rydberg's poem Tomten appeared in 1881, and Jenny Nyström's drawings gave the tomte his look. In the game, Gustaf puts a bowl of porridge on the porch for him, and the tomte asks for nothing more.

25 December

Julotta: church before dawn

In Gustaf's childhood in Mora, the whole village set off for church by sleigh long before dawn on Christmas morning, carrying burning torches through the dark. The early service was called julotta. Outside the church, people threw their torches into a big pile, and the fire lit up the snow.

Afterwards everyone raced home by sleigh. People said that whoever got home first would get their harvest in first, too.

A Swedish Christmas in Minnesota

The emigrants brought Christmas with them. In Gustaf's Minnesota there was lutfisk and rice porridge at Christmas, and before dawn the whole family drove to julotta by sleigh, just like in Mora.

In Mora, Minnesota, the Lutheran church held a lutfisk supper every December, and half the county came: Swedes, Norwegians and neighbours alike. Lutfisk is dried cod soaked in lye for days and then in water. Cooked, it is soft and wobbles a little. Swedish-Americans like their lutfisk with allspice, while Norwegian-Americans often eat it plain with butter.

The tree was decorated the Swedish way, with straw stars and red apples, and the American grandchildren added shiny tinsel.

13 January

Saint Knut's Day: Christmas is over

On 13 January, Saint Knut's Day, Swedes dance round the tree one last time and carry it out. Christmas is over for another year.
